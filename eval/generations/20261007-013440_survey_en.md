# Survey of Sparse Mixture‑of‑Experts Architectures for Large‑Scale Language Modeling

## Introduction  
Sparse mixture‑of‑experts (MoE) layers have emerged as a promising way to enlarge model capacity without a proportional increase in computation. By activating only a small subset of expert sub‑networks for each token, researchers have been able to train models with billions or even trillions of parameters while keeping FLOPs per token roughly constant. This survey reviews three representative works—**Outrageously Large Neural Networks** [1], **Switch Transformers** [2], and **Mixtral of Experts** [3]—focusing on their problem statements, architectural innovations, experimental setups, empirical results, and reported limitations.

## Background  
Early conditional‑computation ideas aimed to decouple parameter count from compute cost, but practical implementations were hampered by routing overhead, load‑balancing difficulties, and instability during training. The MoE paradigm addresses these issues by introducing a trainable gating network that selects a sparse set of experts per input. Over the last few years, successive designs have refined routing (binary vs soft, single vs multiple experts), reduced communication in distributed settings, and combined MoE with modern decoder‑only or encoder‑decoder backbones. The three papers surveyed illustrate this evolution from dense LSTM stacks [1] to Transformer‑based sparse routers [2] and finally to highly‑optimized decoder‑only SMoE models for inference‑efficient deployment [3].

## Approaches  

### 1. Outrageously Large Neural Networks: The Sparsely‑Gated Mixture‑of‑Experts Layer (2017) [1]  
**Problem.** Conditional computation was proposed to increase capacity without linearly scaling compute, yet algorithmic and performance bottlenecks prevented large‑scale realization. The authors sought to demonstrate >1000× capacity gains with minor computational overhead.  

**Method.** A sparsely‑gated MoE layer is inserted between stacked LSTM layers. The MoE consists of up to 4096 feed‑forward experts, each ~1 M parameters. A trainable gating network makes deterministic binary decisions, activating exactly four experts per input token. Both flat and hierarchical expert organisations are explored.  

**Experimental set‑up.** Experiments use the 1 Billion‑Word Language Modeling benchmark (≈829 M tokens, vocab ≈ 793 k). Baselines are stacked LSTMs with 2 M–151 M parameters. MoE models vary in expert count (4, 32, 256, 1024, 4096) while keeping ≈8 M ops/timestep, except two high‑capacity MoEs (≈4 B parameters) with larger compute budgets. Training runs on 16–32 Tesla K40 GPUs (TensorFlow). Evaluation metrics: test perplexity, ops/timestep, and TFLOPS/GPU.  

**Results.** The 4096‑expert MoE reduces test perplexity by **24 %** relative to computationally matched baselines. Two MoEs with ≈4 B parameters surpass the best published benchmark result while using only **6 %** of the computation. Low‑compute MoEs achieve 0.74–0.90 TFLOPS/GPU versus 1.07–1.29 TFLOPS/GPU for dense baselines.  

**Limitations.** The paper does not explicitly discuss limitations; no analysis of routing stability, scaling beyond 4 K experts, or inference latency is provided.  

### 2. Switch Transformers: Scaling to Trillion Parameter Models with Simple and Efficient Sparsity (2021) [2]  
**Problem.** Scaling language models to billions or trillions of parameters while keeping compute constant is hampered by MoE complexity, communication overhead, and training instability. The goal is a simpler, more efficient routing scheme that enables stable training of trillion‑parameter sparse models.  

**Method.** The Switch Transformer uses a simplified MoE where each token is routed to **a single expert** via a lightweight router, eliminating the second‑expert gating component of earlier MoEs. Data‑parallel expert allocation reduces inter‑device communication. Additional techniques include selective bfloat16 precision, a specialized initialization for large expert pools, and stronger expert‑load regularization. The design also facilitates low‑precision training and knowledge distillation to dense student models.  

**Experimental set‑up.** Models are built on T5‑Base (223 M) and T5‑Large (739 M) backbones with FLOP‑matched Switch variants. Pre‑training uses an improved C4 corpus (550 k steps, 576 B tokens, batch ≈ 1 M tokens). Fine‑tuning covers 16 k steps on a suite of 12 downstream tasks (GLUE, SuperGLUE, CNN/DailyMail, XSum, SQuAD, ARC, Natural Questions, Web Questions, Trivia QA, Winogrande, Adversarial NLI). Metrics: GLUE/SuperGLUE averages, ROUGE‑2, EM/F1, and task‑specific scores. Comparisons are made against dense T5‑Base and T5‑Large.  

**Results.** Switch Transformers achieve up to **7×** pre‑training speedup with identical FLOPs per token as dense models. Trillion‑parameter Switch models on the Colossal Clean Crawled Corpus obtain a **4×** speedup over T5‑XXL. In multilingual experiments across 101 languages, **91 %** of languages see >4× speedup versus mT5‑Base. Distillation can compress the model by up to **99 %** while retaining **30 %** of the teacher’s quality gains. Even configurations with only two experts run on limited hardware and outperform dense model‑parallel baselines on the speed‑accuracy Pareto curve.  

**Limitations.** The authors do not report explicit limitations; potential issues such as routing bias or expert under‑utilization are not quantified.  

### 3. Mixtral of Experts (2024) [3]  
**Problem.** Deploying extremely large language models is constrained by inference cost and latency, especially at low batch sizes. Mixtral seeks to retain the benefits of massive parameter counts (≈47 B) while activating only a small fraction (≈13 B) during inference, thereby reducing latency and improving throughput.  

**Method.** Mixtral adopts a decoder‑only architecture identical to Mistral 7B, but each Transformer layer contains **8 feed‑forward expert blocks**. A router selects **two experts** per token per layer; the token is processed through both and their outputs are summed. Training uses a 32 k token context window on multilingual data, followed by instruction fine‑tuning with supervised learning and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) for the Instruct variant.  

**Experimental set‑up.** Mixtral 8×7 B (effective 47 B parameters, 13 B active) is compared against Llama 2 70 B, GPT‑3.5, Mistral 7 B, and Llama 1 34 B on a comprehensive benchmark suite: commonsense (HellaSwag, Winogrande, PIQA, etc.), world knowledge (NaturalQuestions, TriviaQA), reading comprehension (BoolQ, QuAC), mathematics (GSM8K 8‑shot, MATH 4‑shot), code (HumanEval 0‑shot, MBPP 3‑shot), and aggregated metrics (MMLU 5‑shot, BBH 3‑shot, AGI Eval 3‑5‑shot). Human‑evaluation datasets (BBQ, BOLD) assess the Instruct variant.  

**Results.** Mixtral 8×7 B matches or exceeds Llama 2 70 B and GPT‑3.5 across **all** benchmarks, with especially large gains in mathematics, code generation, and multilingual tasks (French, German, Spanish, Italian). The Instruct variant surpasses GPT‑3.5 Turbo, Claude‑2.1, Gemini Pro, and Llama 2 70 B chat models on human‑rated benchmarks, while exhibiting reduced bias and a more balanced sentiment profile. Despite using only 13 B active parameters per token—**5× fewer** than Llama 2 70 B—Mixtral delivers higher cost‑performance efficiency.  

**Limitations.** No explicit limitations are reported; the paper does not discuss routing overhead, expert load balancing, or scaling beyond the 8‑expert per layer configuration.  

## Comparison and Discussion  

### Design  
| Dimension | Outrageously Large NN [1] | Switch Transformers [2] | Mixtral [3] |
|-----------|---------------------------|--------------------------|-------------|
| Backbone | Stacked LSTMs | T5 encoder‑decoder (Base/Large) | Decoder‑only (Mistral 7B) |
| Expert count per layer | Up to 4096 experts (global) | Up to many experts, but **single‑expert** routing per token | 8 experts per layer |
| Experts selected per token | Exactly 4 (binary gating) | Exactly 1 (lightweight router) | Exactly 2 (additive combination) |
| Routing complexity | Deterministic binary gating; hierarchical options | Simple softmax over experts → argmax; data‑parallel allocation | Router network; selects two experts per layer |

All three designs aim for extreme sparsity, but they differ in granularity: [1] uses a large global pool with multiple active experts; [2] reduces to a single expert to cut communication; [3] balances between them with two experts per token, enabling richer representation while still limiting compute.  

### Training and Data  
- **Scale of pre‑training data:** [1] uses a single 1 B‑word benchmark; [2] pre‑trains on 576 B tokens from an improved C4 corpus; [3] employs multilingual data with a 32 k context window (exact token count not disclosed).  
- **Training stability techniques:** [1] does not discuss special tricks; [2] introduces bfloat16 precision, specialized initialization, and strong expert regularization; [3] adds instruction fine‑tuning and DPO for the chat variant.  
- **Hardware:** [1] trains on 16–32 Tesla K40 GPUs; [2] relies on large TPU pods for trillion‑parameter training; [3] reports no specific hardware but inference focuses on cost efficiency.  

### Efficiency (Compute & Inference)  
- **FLOPs per token:** All three keep FLOPs roughly constant relative to dense baselines.  
- **Throughput:** [1] reports 0.74–0.90 TFLOPS/GPU for low‑compute MoEs versus 1.07–1.29 TFLOPS/GPU for dense LSTMs. [2] claims up to **7×** pre‑training speedup and **4×** speedup for trillion‑parameter models; [3] emphasizes inference cost reduction (13 B active vs 47 B total, 5× fewer active parameters).  
- **Communication overhead:** Reduced in [2] by data‑parallel expert allocation and single‑expert routing; not quantified in [1] or [3].  

### Results (Accuracy / Perplexity)  
- **Language modeling:** [1] achieves 24 % lower perplexity than matched baselines; high‑capacity MoEs beat the best published result on the 1 B‑word benchmark.  
- **Downstream tasks:** [2] improves GLUE/SuperGLUE averages and provides consistent speed‑accuracy gains across 12 tasks; [3] matches or exceeds Llama 2 70 B and GPT‑3.5 on a wide range of benchmarks, with notable superiority in math and code.  
- **Human evaluation:** Only [3] reports human‑rated bias and sentiment improvements.  

### Limitations  
All three papers omit explicit limitation discussions, which makes it difficult to assess issues such as expert under‑utilization, routing bias, or scalability beyond the reported configurations. The lack of reported inference latency for [1] and [2] contrasts with the deployment focus of [3].  

## Open Problems  
1. **Load Balancing and Expert Utilization.** While [2] introduces regularization, quantitative analyses of expert load distribution are missing across all works.  
2. **Routing Stability at Extreme Scale.** Training trillion‑parameter MoEs remains sensitive to initialization and precision; systematic studies are needed.  
3. **Inference Latency on Commodity Hardware.** [1] and [2] focus on training efficiency; practical deployment latency, especially for hierarchical MoEs, is an open question.  
4. **Bias and Fairness in Sparse Experts.** Only [3] evaluates bias reduction; systematic methods to audit and mitigate expert‑specific biases are lacking.  
5. **Generalization Across Modalities.** Extending these sparsity techniques to multimodal models (vision‑language, audio) remains largely unexplored.  

## Conclusion  
The three surveyed works illustrate a clear trajectory: from large‑scale sparsely‑gated LSTM MoEs demonstrating massive capacity gains [1], to streamlined single‑expert routing that enables trillion‑parameter training with substantial speedups [2], and finally to a highly efficient decoder‑only SMoE that delivers state‑of‑the‑art performance with dramatically reduced active parameters [3]. Despite impressive empirical gains, all papers leave critical aspects—load balancing, bias analysis, and detailed inference profiling—under‑reported. Addressing these open problems will be essential for the next generation of scalable, trustworthy, and deployment‑ready sparse language models.
