# Sparse Mixture‑of‑Experts for Language Modeling: Evolution, Challenges, and Future Directions

## Introduction

The rapid growth of language‑model capabilities has highlighted a fundamental tension between model capacity and computational cost, prompting researchers to explore conditional computation as a way to decouple the two. Early work demonstrated that sparsely‑gated mixture‑of‑experts (MoE) layers can increase parameter count by orders of magnitude while keeping per‑example work modest, but practical obstacles such as routing complexity, communication overhead, and training instability have limited large‑scale adoption [1]. Recent advances have sought to overcome these hurdles by simplifying the routing mechanism, improving expert regularization, and enabling efficient low‑precision training, thereby making trillion‑parameter sparse models feasible [2]. The most recent generation of models further refines this paradigm by combining high‑capacity expert banks with lightweight routers that activate only a small subset of experts at inference time, delivering substantial speed‑up and lower latency without sacrificing performance [3].

This survey focuses on the evolution of sparse MoE techniques for natural‑language processing, from the first conditional‑computation proposals to the state‑of‑the‑art decoder‑only architectures. It examines the algorithmic innovations that make conditional computation practical, the engineering solutions that reduce communication and memory demands, and the training strategies that ensure stability at massive scales. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. The **Background** section reviews the theoretical motivations for conditional computation and summarizes key early designs. The **Approaches** section details representative architectures, including the sparsely‑gated MoE layer, the Switch Transformer, and Mixtral, highlighting their routing strategies, expert configurations, and training pipelines. The **Comparison and Discussion** section evaluates these methods along dimensions such as model capacity, computational overhead, scalability, and inference efficiency. The **Open Problems** section identifies remaining challenges, including expert imbalance, routing bias, and deployment constraints. Finally, the **Conclusion** synthesizes the findings and outlines promising directions for future research.

## Background

Conditional computation provides a framework for increasing a model’s representational capacity without a proportional rise in the amount of arithmetic performed for each input.  The central idea is to endow a network with many parameter‑rich sub‑components—often called experts—and to activate only a small subset of them for a given example.  In this way the total number of parameters can grow to the order of billions or trillions while the per‑token FLOP count remains comparable to that of a dense model.  The mechanism that chooses which experts to use is typically a lightweight gating network, and the overall architecture is referred to as a mixture‑of‑experts (MoE) or sparsely‑gated MoE layer.

Early work on sparsely‑gated MoE layers demonstrated both the promise and the practical difficulties of this approach.  Shazeer et al. introduced a layer that placed thousands of feed‑forward experts between stacked LSTM units and used a trainable gate to select exactly four experts for each input token [1].  By enforcing binary, deterministic routing decisions the authors showed that model capacity could be enlarged by more than three orders of magnitude with only a modest increase in computation.  Nevertheless, the study highlighted algorithmic challenges such as load balancing among experts and the need for efficient parallel implementations.

Subsequent research has sought to simplify the routing logic and to address the instability that can arise when many experts compete for the same data.  The Switch Transformer streamlined the MoE design by assigning each token to a single expert rather than a mixture, thereby eliminating the second‑expert computation and reducing inter‑device communication overhead [2].  The authors complemented this architectural change with practical training tricks—including a specialized initialization for large expert collections, selective bfloat16 precision, and stronger regularization of the router—to achieve stable training of models with up to a trillion parameters.

The most recent generation of sparse models continues this trajectory by combining a decoder‑only transformer backbone with a modest number of experts per layer while preserving high inference efficiency.  Mixtral of Experts adopts the Mistral 7B architecture and augments each feed‑forward block with eight expert modules; a router selects two experts per token, and their outputs are summed to form the final layer representation [3].  This design enables a 47‑billion‑parameter model to run with only 13 billion active parameters during inference, delivering lower latency at small batch sizes and higher throughput when the batch grows.  The training regimen also incorporates a large context window, multilingual pretraining, and instruction‑following fine‑tuning, illustrating how modern sparse MoE systems integrate both architectural and data‑centric advances.

Taken together, these works define the essential terminology—experts, gating/router networks, sparsity level, and load‑balancing—and establish the chronological progression from early sparsely‑gated layers to today’s highly optimized, decoder‑only sparse transformers.  Understanding these concepts is prerequisite for evaluating the specific approaches that follow in the survey.

## Approaches

### [1] Outrageously Large Neural Networks: The Sparsely-Gated Mixture-of-Experts Layer (2017)

The work of Shazeer et al. (2017) tackles the core obstacle that conditional‑computation schemes have historically faced: while increasing model capacity can improve accuracy, doing so without a proportional rise in arithmetic cost has proved difficult in practice. The authors therefore set out to demonstrate that a sparsely‑gated mixture‑of‑experts (MoE) layer can provide more than a thousand‑fold increase in parameter count while adding only a modest amount of extra computation per training example [1].

The proposed MoE layer consists of thousands of independent feed‑forward experts, each containing roughly one million parameters. A trainable gating network receives the current hidden representation and selects exactly four experts for every input token; the selection is binary, sparse, and deterministic. By inserting this MoE module convolutionally between two stacked LSTM layers, the overall architecture retains the sequential modeling power of recurrent networks while allowing the bulk of the parameters to remain dormant for any given example. The authors explore two routing topologies: a flat gate that chooses among all experts directly, and a hierarchical gate that first picks a subset of expert groups before selecting individual experts within the chosen group. The key design choices—fixed sparsity of four active experts, deterministic gating, and placement of the MoE between LSTM layers—are intended to keep the forward‑pass cost roughly constant across models of vastly different capacities.

Experiments are carried out on the 1 Billion‑Word Language Modeling benchmark, which contains about 829 M words and a vocabulary of 793 471 tokens. Baseline systems are conventional stacked LSTM models whose parameter counts range from 2 M to 151 M. The MoE variants vary the total number of experts (4, 32, 256, 1 024, 4 096) while keeping the compute budget fixed at approximately 8 M multiply‑and‑add operations per timestep (ops/timestep). For the low‑compute regime, all models use the same two‑LSTM backbone; each expert contributes about one million parameters, and exactly four experts are active per token. The hierarchical configurations include 256, 1 024, and 4 096 experts, whereas the flat configurations use 4, 32, and 256 experts. Training runs on clusters of 16–32 Tesla K40 GPUs using TensorFlow, and computational efficiency is reported in TFLOPS per GPU.

The results show a clear capacity‑vs‑perplexity trade‑off. The MoE model with 4 096 experts achieves a test perplexity that is 24 % lower than that of computationally matched baselines, confirming that massive parameter growth can translate into substantial quality gains without increasing ops/timestep. In a second set of experiments the authors scale two models to roughly 4 billion total parameters (by enlarging the LSTM layers and using fewer but larger experts). Both high‑capacity MoEs surpass the previously best published result on the benchmark while consuming only 6 % of the computation required by that prior state‑of‑the‑art system. Regarding efficiency, baseline LSTMs attain 1.07–1.29 TFLOPS/GPU, whereas the low‑compute MoE models attain 0.74–0.90 TFLOPS/GPU (the 4‑expert model under‑utilises the hardware). These figures illustrate that the added experts contribute between 37 % and 46 % of the total floating‑point operations, yet the overall throughput remains competitive.

The authors do not explicitly enumerate limitations in the paper; the discussion focuses on the achieved gains and the computational profile of the models. Consequently, any potential drawbacks—such as the reduced TFLOPS/GPU observed for the MoE variants or challenges in scaling the gating mechanism beyond the explored expert counts—are left unaddressed in the original text.

### [2] Switch Transformers: Scaling to Trillion Parameter Models with Simple and Efficient Sparsity (2021)

The Switch Transformer addresses the core scalability dilemma of modern language models: how to increase parameter count to improve capacity while keeping the per‑token computational cost constant.  Dense Transformers allocate the same weight matrix to every token, so expanding the model inevitably raises FLOPs and memory usage, limiting practical deployment on current accelerators.  By contrast, mixture‑of‑experts (MoE) architectures activate only a subset of experts for each example, allowing a trillion‑parameter model to be trained with the same FLOPs per token as a much smaller dense baseline.  However, earlier MoE systems suffered from complex routing, high communication overhead, and instability when many experts competed for the same data, which prevented widespread adoption.  The authors therefore set out to simplify the MoE routing, reduce inter‑device communication, and introduce training tricks that stabilize large‑scale sparse training [2].

The proposed method, called the Switch Transformer, simplifies the classic MoE gating network by allowing each token to be routed to exactly one expert instead of the top‑k experts used in prior work.  The router is a lightweight linear layer followed by a softmax, and the second expert is removed entirely, which eliminates the need for a load‑balancing loss that depends on multiple assignments.  Because each token is processed by a single expert, the communication pattern collapses to a binary gather/scatter operation that can be implemented efficiently with data‑parallel expert allocation; all cores are assigned to the data‑parallel dimension, and the number of experts equals the number of data‑parallel partitions.  To further improve stability, the authors introduce an initialization scheme that scales the expert weights with the square root of the number of experts, employ selective bfloat16 precision for most matrix multiplications, and increase the regularization term that penalizes expert over‑use.  The design also enables straightforward distillation: a dense student can be trained on the soft targets of a sparse teacher, achieving up to a 99 % reduction in parameter count while retaining roughly 30 % of the teacher’s quality gains.

Experimental evaluation follows a two‑stage protocol.  In the pre‑training phase the authors build Switch variants that are FLOP‑matched to T5‑Base (223 M parameters) and T5‑Large (739 M parameters) but contain many more parameters because of the expert layers.  Pre‑training uses an improved C4 corpus, processing 1,048,576 tokens per batch for 550 k steps, which corresponds to 576 B tokens overall.  The same hyper‑parameters are used for both dense and sparse models, except that Switch layers receive a higher dropout rate (0.4 versus 0.1 for other layers).  Fine‑tuning is performed on a broad suite of 12 tasks that cover classification (GLUE, SuperGLUE), summarization (CNN/Dailymail, XSum), extractive and closed‑book question answering (SQuAD, Natural Questions, WebQuestions, Trivia QA, ARC), common‑sense reasoning (Winogrande), and adversarial natural‑language inference.  Each fine‑tuning run uses a batch size of 1 M tokens for 16 k steps; validation is evaluated every 200 steps and the peak score is reported.  Metrics include the average GLUE/SuperGLUE score, ROUGE‑2 for summarization, and exact‑match/F1 for the QA sets.

Results demonstrate that the Switch architecture delivers substantial speed‑up without sacrificing accuracy.  During pre‑training, Switch models achieve up to a 7× increase in token‑per‑second throughput while maintaining the same FLOPs per token as dense T5 baselines.  When scaled to a trillion‑parameter configuration on the Colossal Clean Crawled Corpus, the Switch model trains four times faster than the dense T5‑XXL baseline.  In multilingual experiments covering 101 languages, 91 % of the languages experience at least a 4× speed‑up over the mT5‑Base model, and quality improves uniformly across the language set.  On downstream tasks, FLOP‑matched Switch models match or exceed their dense counterparts; for example, the Switch‑Large variant attains higher average GLUE and SuperGLUE scores than T5‑Large while using the same compute budget.  Distillation experiments confirm that a dense student can retain a significant portion of the sparse teacher’s advantage, shrinking the model by up to 99 % with only a modest loss in performance.

The paper does not explicitly list limitations, but the authors acknowledge that the benefits diminish when the number of experts becomes very large, as shown by a plateau in scaling curves (Figure 4).  Moreover, the simplified routing assumes that a single expert per token is sufficient, which may constrain expressivity for tasks that could benefit from richer mixtures of experts.  Finally, the presented results focus on transformer‑style language models; extending the Switch design to other modalities or to encoder‑only architectures remains an open question.

### [3] Mixtral of Experts (2024)

Mixtral 8×7B tackles the fundamental problem of scaling language‑model capacity while keeping inference cost and latency low.  The authors observe that conventional dense models require a one‑to‑one correspondence between the number of parameters and the amount of arithmetic performed for each token, which hampers deployment of very large models.  By adopting a sparse mixture‑of‑experts (SMoE) architecture, Mixtral enables every token to draw on a total of 47 B parameters but to activate only a small subset—approximately 13 B active parameters—during inference, thereby reducing compute proportional to the active‑parameter count [3].

The architecture follows a decoder‑only design that mirrors the Mistral 7B backbone, yet each transformer layer contains eight distinct feed‑forward expert blocks.  A lightweight router network selects two experts for every token at each layer; the token is processed by these two experts and their outputs are combined additively.  This routing scheme preserves the expressivity of a much larger parameter pool while limiting the per‑token arithmetic to the chosen experts.  Training proceeds with a 32 k‑token context window on multilingual pre‑training data, and the authors further fine‑tune an “Instruct” variant using supervised instruction data followed by Direct Preference Optimization.  The design choices—fixed number of experts per layer, two‑expert routing, and large context size—are intended to balance model capacity, routing stability, and the ability to retrieve information from very long sequences [3].

To assess the effectiveness of the approach, the authors evaluate Mixtral 8×7B against several strong baselines, including Llama 2 70 B, GPT‑3.5‑Turbo, Mistral 7 B, and Llama 1 34 B.  The benchmark suite spans commonsense reasoning (HellaSwag, Winogrande, PIQA, SIQA, OpenbookQA, ARC‑Easy, ARC‑Challenge, CommonsenseQA), world knowledge (NaturalQuestions, TriviaQA), reading comprehension (BoolQ, QuAC), mathematics (GSM8K 8‑shot, MATH 4‑shot), code generation (HumanEval 0‑shot, MBPP 3‑shot), and aggregated metrics such as MMLU 5‑shot, BBH 3‑shot, and AGI‑Eval 3‑5‑shot.  All evaluations are performed with the authors’ own pipeline to ensure a fair comparison.  Results show that Mixtral matches or exceeds the performance of Llama 2 70 B and GPT‑3.5 across the entire benchmark collection.  In particular, Mixtral delivers superior scores on mathematics and code generation tasks and achieves markedly higher accuracy on multilingual benchmarks in French, German, Spanish, and Italian.  When measured against active‑parameter count, Mixtral’s 13 B active parameters—five times fewer than Llama 2 70 B—yield higher cost‑performance efficiency, while the total 47 B parameter memory footprint remains smaller than the dense 70 B model.  The Instruct variant further surpasses GPT‑3.5‑Turbo, Claude‑2.1, Gemini Pro, and Llama 2 70 B chat models on human‑evaluation tests such as BBQ and BOLD, and it exhibits reduced bias and a more balanced sentiment profile [3].

The paper does not articulate explicit limitations of the Mixtral approach; the authors note only that the analysis of efficiency focuses on active‑parameter count and does not account for memory costs or hardware utilization, and that the routing mechanism introduces additional overhead that is best amortized in batched workloads [3].

## Comparison and Discussion

### Design  
The original sparsely‑gated MoE layer introduced by Shazeer et al. (2017) employs a deterministic binary gating network that activates exactly four experts per token and places the MoE between stacked LSTM layers [1]. In contrast, the Switch Transformer (2021) simplifies the routing by assigning each token to a single expert via a lightweight router and removes the second expert from the gating decision [2]. Mixtral of Experts (2024) adopts a decoder‑only transformer backbone similar to Mistral 7B, but each feed‑forward block contains eight experts and the router selects two experts per token, combining their outputs additively [3]. Thus, the three works share the core idea of conditional computation but differ in the number of experts activated per token (four, one, and two respectively) and in the surrounding architecture (LSTM‑based, encoder‑decoder T5, and decoder‑only transformer). The trend shows a move from a more complex, multi‑expert gating toward simpler, single‑ or dual‑expert routing to reduce communication overhead while preserving the benefits of large parameter pools.

### Training and Data  
Shazeer et al. trained MoE models on the 1 Billion‑Word Language Modeling benchmark using 16–32 Tesla K40 GPUs and TensorFlow, focusing exclusively on a monolingual corpus of English news [1]. The Switch Transformer scaled up to trillion‑parameter models by pre‑training on the Colossal Clean Crawled Corpus (C4) with 576 B tokens and then fine‑tuning on a diverse set of downstream tasks such as GLUE, SuperGLUE, and numerous QA and summarisation benchmarks; training employed bfloat16 precision and specialist initialization for massive expert counts [2]. Mixtral was trained with a 32 k‑token context window on multilingual pre‑training data, followed by supervised instruction fine‑tuning and Direct Preference Optimization for its Instruct variant [3]. While the early work relied on a single English benchmark, the later systems exploit vastly larger, multilingual corpora and incorporate task‑specific fine‑tuning, reflecting an evolution toward broader data coverage and more sophisticated training pipelines.

### Efficiency  
All three papers report that sparse activation yields substantial computational savings relative to dense models of comparable parameter count. Shazeer et al. demonstrated that a MoE with 4 096 experts achieved a 24 % perplexity reduction while using only 6 % of the compute of a dense baseline, with low‑compute MoEs operating at 0.74–0.90 TFLOPS/GPU compared to 1.07–1.29 TFLOPS/GPU for dense LSTMs [1]. The Switch Transformer further reduced communication overhead by routing each token to a single expert, which enabled up to a 7× pre‑training speedup while keeping FLOPs per token constant; it also achieved a 4× speedup over T5‑XXL in trillion‑parameter regimes [2]. Mixtral reports that only 13 B of its 47 B parameters are active per token—approximately a five‑fold reduction in active parameters compared with a dense 70 B model—yet it delivers higher throughput and lower latency, especially at small batch sizes [3]. Collectively, the results illustrate a consistent trade‑off: as routing becomes simpler, communication costs drop and training/inference speed improves, while maintaining or even enhancing model quality.

### Results  
The empirical outcomes of the three systems confirm that conditional computation can deliver state‑of‑the‑art performance with far fewer operations. Shazeer et al.’s 4 096‑expert MoE lowered test perplexity on the Billion‑Word benchmark relative to all baselines with comparable compute [1]. Switch Transformers matched dense T5 models on a wide suite of NLP tasks while providing significant speedups; they also outperformed dense model‑parallel baselines on the FLOP‑accuracy Pareto frontier and transferred well to multilingual settings, benefitting 91 % of languages with at least a 4× speedup [2]. Mixtral 8×7B matched or exceeded the performance of much larger dense models such as Llama 2 70B and GPT‑3.5 across benchmarks spanning commonsense reasoning, mathematics, code generation, and multilingual tasks; its Instruct variant further surpassed leading chat models in human evaluations while using far fewer active parameters [3]. These findings collectively support the claim that sparsely activated experts can achieve superior cost‑performance compared with dense scaling.

### Limitations  
Although each paper focuses on the advantages of sparse MoE architectures, none provides an explicit limitation analysis. Shazeer et al. do not discuss potential instability arising from expert imbalance or routing overhead, despite noting practical challenges in early large‑scale attempts [1]. The Switch Transformer paper omits a detailed examination of the impact of routing granularity on model robustness or the scalability limits of the single‑expert design [2]. Mixtral likewise lacks a formal discussion of its constraints, such as possible degradation when the number of active experts is reduced or the effects of multilingual pre‑training on low‑resource languages [3]. The absence of explicit limitation sections suggests that future work should investigate expert utilization efficiency, training stability, and fairness across languages and domains.

## Open Problems

The impressive gains reported by the early sparsely‑gated MoE layer [1] and the subsequent Switch Transformer [2] and Mixtral of Experts [3] have nevertheless exposed several research fronts that remain only partially addressed.  

First, the routing mechanisms that decide which expert processes each token are still vulnerable to instability and load‑balancing failures. While Switch Transformers simplify routing by selecting a single expert per token, the original MoE design already showed that many experts can compete for the same data, leading to “expert collapse” in practice. Developing routing strategies that guarantee both computational predictability and equitable expert utilization—especially when the number of experts grows to the trillion‑parameter regime—remains an open challenge.  

Second, the interaction between sparsity and model robustness has not been thoroughly explored. The current literature highlights performance improvements on standard language‑modeling benchmarks and multilingual corpora, yet it provides limited insight into how sparse activation patterns affect adversarial vulnerability, out‑of‑distribution generalisation, or long‑range dependency handling. Systematic evaluations of robustness across diverse downstream tasks would clarify whether the efficiency gains come at the cost of reliability.  

Third, the integration of sparse MoE layers with other architectural innovations, such as retrieval‑augmented generation or vision‑language multimodality, is still in its infancy. Existing work focuses on decoder‑only transformers for pure text generation; extending sparsity to encoder‑decoder or multimodal settings may uncover new bottlenecks in routing latency, memory fragmentation, or cross‑modal expert coordination.  

Fourth, although Switch Transformers demonstrate that distillation can shrink a trillion‑parameter teacher to a compact student while preserving a fraction of the quality gains, the distilled models still lag behind their dense counterparts on many specialised benchmarks. Designing distillation pipelines that more faithfully transfer the specialised knowledge of individual experts—perhaps by preserving expert‑specific representations rather than averaging them—constitutes a promising direction.  

Fifth, bias mitigation and fairness remain only cursorily addressed. Mixtral of Experts reports reduced biases and a more balanced sentiment profile compared with larger dense models, yet the mechanisms by which sparsity influences bias propagation are not elucidated. Understanding whether selective expert activation amplifies or attenuates societal biases, and developing training objectives that enforce fairness across the expert ensemble, are critical for responsible deployment.  

Finally, evaluation metrics themselves may need refinement. Current studies rely heavily on perplexity reductions, speed‑up factors, and benchmark scores, which do not capture the full spectrum of user‑centric criteria such as latency variance, energy consumption on heterogeneous hardware, or interpretability of expert behaviours. Establishing standardized, holistic metrics that reflect both efficiency and qualitative aspects of model output will enable more meaningful comparisons among sparse architectures.  

Addressing these open problems will be essential for advancing sparse mixture‑of‑experts models from impressive research prototypes to robust, fair, and versatile components of future language‑understanding systems.

## Conclusion

The survey has traced how conditional computation has become a central strategy for enlarging language‑model capacity while keeping inference cost modest. Early sparsely‑gated MoE layers demonstrated that thousands of expert feed‑forward networks can be activated selectively, yielding a clear capacity‑vs‑perplexity benefit without a proportional increase in FLOPs [1]. Subsequent work refined the routing mechanisms and introduced architectural simplifications that improved scalability; the Switch Transformer showed that a single active expert per token can preserve most of the performance gains while dramatically reducing the routing overhead [2]. The most recent decoder‑only models, exemplified by Mixtral of Experts, combine a modest number of experts with a transformer backbone, confirming that sparse activation remains effective in contemporary large‑scale NLP systems [3].

Across these developments, the literature consistently highlights two recurring themes. First, the trade‑off between model capacity and computational efficiency is mitigated by sparse expert selection, confirming the original promise of conditional computation. Second, practical challenges such as load‑balancing, routing instability, and integration with other architectural components have persisted despite incremental improvements. By synthesizing the contributions of the three seminal works, this survey underscores that sparse MoE techniques have matured into a viable paradigm for building ever larger language models, yet they continue to demand careful engineering of routing and expert management to fully realize their potential.
